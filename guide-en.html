01 / THE TRADITION

What is omikuji?

Omikuji are fortune slips found at Japanese shrines and temples. Alongside an overall fortune, a slip may offer guidance on wishes, a person or message you are waiting for, and travel. Some also include a poem. The format and contents vary from one shrine or temple to another.

The Association of Shinto Shrines emphasizes reading the words as guidance for daily life rather than focusing only on whether a fortune is good or bad. The digital fortunes on this site are original creations inspired by the format; they are not actual shrine fortunes or a substitute for religious practice.

A note before you begin

These original fortunes are intended for entertainment and reflection. For important health, legal or financial decisions, consult an appropriately qualified professional.

02 / HOW TO READ

Read the words, not just the grade

A label such as daikichi (大吉), kichi (吉), chūkichi (中吉), shōkichi (小吉), suekichi (末吉) or kyō (凶) indicates an overall fortune. The order and number of grades differ by shrine or temple, so the same label does not necessarily have the same rank everywhere. Follow any explanation given on site.

大吉DAIKICHI

A very favourable fortune. Even with a good result, read the advice on the slip.

吉KICHI

A favourable fortune. Its position relative to other grades varies by place.

末吉SUEKICHI

A grade often read with the future in mind. Consider the wording on your own slip.

凶KYŌ

Rather than focusing only on an unfavourable label, read the guidance that accompanies it.

For example, Sensō-ji’s official FAQ encourages those who draw kyō not to be afraid, but to live with patience and sincerity. That is Sensō-ji’s own guidance, not a universal rule for every shrine or temple.

04 / VISITING WITH RESPECT

Visiting respectfully

  1. Follow local guidance. Shrines and temples have distinct beliefs and customs, and the way to draw a fortune may vary.
  2. Tie it in the designated place or take it home. The Association of Shinto Shrines says taking a slip home is acceptable. Follow any different instructions at the location.
  3. Care for trees and facilities. Use the designated tying area where one is provided, and leave the surroundings tidy.
  4. Check prices and opening times directly. Consult notices at the site or its official website for current information.
05 / BEFORE YOU VISIT

A closer look: Sensō-ji

Sensō-ji in Asakusa, Tokyo, is a Buddhist temple, not a Shinto shrine. Its official FAQ calls its omikuji Kannon Hyakusen (観音百籤) and asks visitors to return the numbered stick to its container. It also asks visitors who draw kyō to tie their slip in the designated place.

This description is based on official information; it is not a first-hand visit report. Details and local rules can change, so check the official website before visiting.
